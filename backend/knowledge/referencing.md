# Referencing and citation

> Curated guidance for the ShefGuide prototype. ShefGuide serves
> international students across UK higher education generally, not one
> institution. Harvard, APA, and IEEE are shared conventions used across
> most UK universities, illustrated below using one university library's
> published guide as a concrete, citable example — not because the
> convention belongs to that institution. Departments vary in the exact
> style required, so the module handbook or the student's own library
> referencing guide is always authoritative for a specific piece of work.

## Why referencing matters

Referencing shows where an idea came from, lets a reader verify it, and
separates the student's own contribution from other people's work. Failure
to reference properly is treated as an academic offence even when there was
no intention to deceive (University of Hull, "Integrity in assessment").

## Harvard

Harvard is an author-date system: a citation in the text gives the author
surname and year, for example (Smith, 2020), with a page number added for
direct quotations, for example (Smith, 2020, p. 42). Three or more authors
are shortened to the first author followed by "et al." A full reference
list appears at the end of the work, ordered alphabetically by author
surname. This is documented, for example, in the University of Sheffield
Library's "Harvard referencing" guide, one of many UK university library
guides describing the same convention.

There are many local variants of Harvard. The version most widely adopted
across UK universities — including Sheffield's own library guide — is
*Cite Them Right* (Pears and Shields), currently in its 10th edition.
Punctuation and italicisation differ between institutions' variants, so
consistency with the student's own department's guide matters more than
any single online example.

## APA (7th edition)

APA is also an author-date system and is common in psychology and the
social sciences. A single-author in-text citation reads "Author Surname
(Year)" or "(Author Surname, Year)"; two authors use "and" outside brackets
and "&" inside; three or more authors are shortened to the first author
plus "et al." The reference list is alphabetical by surname, with a
consistent author–year–title–publisher structure for each entry — as
documented, for example, in the University of Sheffield Library's "APA
referencing 7th edition" guide, one of many equivalent UK university guides.

## IEEE

IEEE is a numeric style common in engineering and computer science. Sources
are numbered in square brackets in the order they first appear in the text,
for example [1], and the same number is reused for every later citation of
that source. The reference list is ordered by that number rather than
alphabetically, with author initials preceding surnames — as documented,
for example, in the University of Sheffield Library's "IEEE referencing"
guide, one of many equivalent UK university guides.

## Paraphrasing and patchwriting

Paraphrasing means putting a specific idea from a source into your own
words while demonstrating genuine understanding of it — not just swapping
out a few words while keeping the original's structure and phrasing intact.
This distinction is a shared convention across UK higher education; the
University of Sheffield's own guidance states it in explicit, quotable
terms as one example: "changing the odd word" without real comprehension is
patchwriting, not paraphrasing, and is treated as a form of plagiarism even
when a citation is present (University of Sheffield, "How to avoid
plagiarism").

## Reference managers

Tools such as Zotero, Mendeley, and EndNote store sources and generate
formatted citations. They reduce mechanical errors but do not guarantee
correctness: output should always be checked against the required style,
particularly for sources with unusual author or publication details.
