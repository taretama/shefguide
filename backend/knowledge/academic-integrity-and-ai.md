# Academic integrity and the use of AI tools

> Curated guidance for the ShefGuide prototype, grounded in QAA's UK-wide
> sector guidance on generative AI, illustrated with one named university's
> published policy as a concrete example (see Provenance below). ShefGuide
> serves international students across UK higher education generally, not
> one institution — AI policy differs between institutions, departments, and
> even individual modules, and it changes frequently. A student's own
> module handbook and assessment brief are always authoritative for that
> student's own work.

## What counts as an academic offence

Commonly recognised offences across UK higher education include plagiarism
(submitting someone else's work or ideas without attribution), contract
cheating (purchasing or commissioning work from an external service or
individual and presenting it as your own), collusion (working with others
on an assessment meant to be completed individually — discussing and
debating ideas is fine, using them uncritically as if they were your own is
not), and fabrication (submitting invented information — quotations,
results, references, or data — presented as real). Intent is often not a
defence: incorrect referencing that makes another author's work look like
your own can be treated as plagiarism even when the omission was
accidental.

As one concrete example of how a UK university states this, the University
of Sheffield defines academic integrity as "a positive commitment to
approaching academic work in a way that is honest, fair, respectful and
responsible," and explicitly lists misusing a generative AI tool as a form
of unintentional plagiarism (University of Sheffield, "Academic
integrity"). Other UK universities define the same offences in their own
wording — the categories above are the common ground between them, not
wording unique to one institution.

## Where generative AI sits

The Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education (QAA), the sector-wide
body that oversees quality and standards across UK higher education,
advises institutions to treat generative AI as an academic integrity
question rather than purely a detection problem, and to categorise
assessments rather than apply one blanket rule: AI prohibited (where the
learning outcome requires unaided work), AI permitted with declaration
(where AI can support the work but must be disclosed), or AI required
(where AI literacy itself is being assessed) (QAA, "QAA advice and
resources on Generative AI"). Individual institutions then set their own
specific policy within that shape.

As one worked example of what an institutional policy built on that shape
actually looks like in practice: the University of Sheffield's policy
permits generative AI as a learning tool only where an assessment's own
guidance explicitly allows it, treats copying GenAI output directly into
submitted work as misconduct, and requires disclosure whenever GenAI has
been used at all (University of Sheffield, "Generative AI and academic
integrity"). This is Sheffield's specific implementation, shown here as an
illustration of what a real policy looks like — a student at a different
UK institution should expect the same broad shape (prohibited / permitted-
with-declaration / required, set per assessment) but must check their own
university's actual policy for the specifics, since these vary
institution to institution.

Because the position varies by institution and by assessment, the safe
default for any student, at any UK university, is to check the brief for
each specific piece of work rather than assume a single rule applies
everywhere.

## A useful distinction: support versus production

A widely applied line, reflected in QAA's own guidance on redesigning
assessment for the "ChatGPT era", is between using a tool to understand a
task and using it to produce the assessed output. Asking a tool to explain
what "critically evaluate" means, or to explain an unfamiliar concept, is
ordinarily support. Asking it to write the essay, or a section of it, for
submission is ordinarily production, and that is what tips into
misconduct. Sheffield's own policy states this distinction in almost
identical terms: "Do not copy and paste content from an output produced by
a GenAI tool directly into your work. Instead, you should use an output as
a way to inform your further research and thinking" (University of
Sheffield, "Generative AI and academic integrity") — offered here as one
institution's concrete wording of a principle that holds more generally.

ShefGuide is designed around this distinction. It explains how to approach
a task, but it will not write assessed work on a student's behalf.

## AI output is not a source

Generative tools can produce fluent text containing invented facts and
fabricated citations. Content produced by such a tool should never be
cited as evidence. Any factual claim it surfaces needs verifying against a
real, locatable source before it appears in submitted work.
