# Academic integrity and the use of AI tools

> Curated general guidance for the ShefGuide prototype. This is the project's
> own knowledge base, not an official University of Sheffield publication.
> AI policy differs sharply between institutions, departments, and even
> individual modules, and it changes frequently. The module handbook and the
> assessment brief are always authoritative.

## What counts as an academic offence

Commonly recognised offences include plagiarism (presenting someone else's
work or ideas as your own), collusion (working with others on an assessment
meant to be individual), falsification of data, and contract cheating
(submitting work produced by someone else, paid or otherwise).

Intent is often not a defence. Poor referencing that makes another author's
work look like your own can be treated as plagiarism even when the omission
was accidental.

## Where generative AI sits

Institutions generally fall into three broad positions, and a student may
encounter all three across different modules in the same term:

1. AI use prohibited for the assessment.
2. AI use permitted for specific supporting activities, such as explaining
   a concept or checking grammar, usually with a declaration.
3. AI use permitted more broadly, with disclosure of how it was used.

Because the position varies, the safe default is to check the brief for each
assessment rather than assume a single university-wide rule.

## A useful distinction: support versus production

A widely applied line is between using a tool to understand a task and using
it to produce the assessed output. Asking a tool to explain what "critically
evaluate" means, or to explain an unfamiliar concept, is ordinarily support.
Asking it to write the essay, or a section of it, for submission is
ordinarily production and is far more likely to breach the rules.

ShefGuide is designed around this distinction. It explains how to approach a
task, but it will not write assessed work on a student's behalf.

## Anxiety about legitimate use

Research on international students in UK higher education reports that
students often worry that legitimate AI use will be misread as misconduct.
Two practical steps reduce this risk: keep a record of how a tool was used,
and follow any declaration process the module specifies. Where a brief is
ambiguous, asking the module leader in writing creates a useful record.

## AI output is not a source

Generative tools can produce fluent text containing invented facts and
fabricated citations. Content produced by such a tool should never be cited
as evidence. Any factual claim it surfaces needs verifying against a real,
locatable source before it appears in submitted work.
